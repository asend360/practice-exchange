All behaviour communicates something. For many pupils with SEND, behaviour that challenges is the clearest signal we have that a need isn't being met — whether that's communication, sensory, emotional or physical. Treating behaviour as information, rather than only as something to stop, leads to better support and fewer repeat incidents.

## Look for the pattern

A single incident tells you little. A pattern tells you a lot. Recording behaviour consistently — what happened just before, what the behaviour looked like, and what happened after — lets you see:

- **when** it happens: time of day, particular lessons, transitions, after lunch;
- **where**: busy corridors, the dining hall, specific rooms;
- **with whom**: certain adults, peers or group sizes;
- **what follows**: does the behaviour lead to escape from a task, attention, or a sensory need being met?

This antecedent–behaviour–consequence approach is the basis of functional behaviour assessment.

## Ask what the behaviour achieves

Most behaviour serves a purpose for the pupil. A pupil who throws work may be escaping a task that is too hard or too loud. A pupil who runs from the classroom may be avoiding a sensory overload. Once you understand the function, you can teach and reward a safer way to meet the same need.

## Adjust the environment first

Small changes often have the biggest effect: a quieter space to start the day, a visual timetable, a warning before transitions, a planned movement break, or work broken into shorter chunks. The DfE's behaviour guidance expects schools to consider whether a pupil's SEND has contributed to their behaviour and to make reasonable adjustments.

## Plan, then review

Bring what you've learned into a behaviour support plan, agreed with the pupil, their family and staff. Include what the pupil finds difficult, early signs that they're becoming distressed, what helps, and how adults should respond. Review it regularly against the incident record: is the behaviour reducing, and is the pupil using the new strategies?

## Keep the pupil's voice in it

Ask the pupil what helps and what makes things harder, in a way that suits their communication. Their answers are often the most useful evidence you have.

## A worked example

Jamal, in Year 4, has an autism diagnosis and limited spoken language. Over three weeks staff log eleven incidents of him pushing over chairs. Each record is brief, but together they show a clear pattern: nine of the eleven happen in the five minutes after the bell for afternoon play, when the class lines up in a narrow corridor next to the dining hall. In most cases an adult then takes him to a quiet room, where he calms quickly.

The function looks like escape — from noise, closeness and an unpredictable wait — and the consequence, removal to a quiet space, is meeting that need. The team tries three changes: Jamal leaves two minutes before the bell with a familiar adult, he carries a "break" card he can show at any time, and the line moves to a wider space. Over the next four weeks there are two incidents, both on days when the plan wasn't followed. The behaviour support plan is updated to make the early exit the default, and the team starts teaching him to use the break card in other settings.

## Common pitfalls

- **Recording only the behaviour.** Without what happened before and after, you can't see the pattern.
- **Changing everything at once.** If five strategies start on the same day, you won't know which one helped.
- **Rewarding the opposite of what you intend.** If removal from class follows the behaviour every time, a pupil seeking escape learns that the behaviour works.
- **Stopping too soon.** Fewer incidents for two weeks is encouraging; keep the plan in place long enough to be sure.

## Questions to discuss with your team

- Do our incident records consistently capture what happened before and after?
- Which pupils have patterns we haven't yet analysed?
- Are reasonable adjustments recorded so we can show what we've tried?
- How do we include the views of pupils who don't use spoken language?

## Quick checklist

- Behaviour is recorded consistently, with what happened before and after.
- Patterns by time, place and people are reviewed regularly.
- The likely function of the behaviour is identified.
- Reasonable adjustments are tried and recorded.
- A support plan is agreed with the pupil and family, and reviewed.
