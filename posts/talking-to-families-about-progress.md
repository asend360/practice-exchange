On 13 November, the Department for Education published a research report on how parental engagement relates to children's outcomes, from the first year of primary school to their early twenties. It draws on three large studies that follow thousands of children over time, linked to their attainment, attendance and later employment records.

Much of it confirms what schools already know: talk, books and reading at home matter. But one finding deserves particular attention from anyone who works with families of pupils with SEND, because it challenges a common assumption about what good communication looks like.

## Clarity, not contact

The report compares two things that are easy to confuse: how *often* schools and families are in touch, and how *clear* the information is that families receive.

Clarity came out well. Parents of Year 11 pupils who said the school's information about their child's progress was "very clear" had children who scored around 6 more Attainment 8 points than those who said it wasn't clear or didn't know, and had around 27% lower odds of persistent absence. Where parents said the school had told them how well it expected their child to do, and how their child was progressing against that, children scored around 6 to 7 more Attainment 8 points and had between 35% and 49% lower odds of persistent absence.

How often school and home were in touch told a different story. Compared with parents who were never in contact with teachers, parents in touch at least once a week had children who scored around 8 points lower, with around three times the odds of persistent absence. Updates sent by the school showed no consistent pattern either way.

The authors are clear that this doesn't mean contact is harmful. Schools and families talk most when something is going wrong, so frequent contact is often a sign of difficulty, not a cause of it. In their words, "high levels of contact may function as indicators of educational difficulty rather than productive engagement".

## Why this matters even more for SEND

For many families of pupils with SEND, that reactive pattern is simply everyday life. The report notes that parents of children with SEN typically have more contact with schools than other parents, and that much of it is about identifying needs, planning and reviewing support, and monitoring progress.

The SEN findings follow the same split. The report draws on two generations of pupils: an older group who were at secondary school between 2012 and 2015, when the highest level of support was a statement of SEN (the forerunner of today's EHCP), and a recent group of Year 8 pupils, where it was an EHCP. For pupils with this level of need, measures such as parents' awareness of school work and their involvement in school life often went with *poorer* outcomes, which the authors read as families becoming more involved when their child is struggling. More structured planning and communication, on the other hand, looked more positive.

The clearest example is planning for the future. Across all pupils, talking at home about Year 10 subject choices went with around 4 more Attainment 8 points. For pupils with a statement, in the older group, the association was around 14 points larger again. The report suggests planning conversations may be particularly important at transition points for pupils with more complex needs.

Two cautions are important. These are associations, not proof of cause: schools that communicate clearly may also be stronger in other ways. The SEN results also come from relatively small groups, and the subject-choices finding comes from the older group, before EHCPs replaced statements in 2014, when SEND law and practice were different. The authors ask for them to be treated cautiously until they're repeated in other data. Even so, the direction is consistent, and it matches what many SENCOs see every day.

## What this looks like in practice

**Tell families what you're aiming for.** For a pupil with SEND, expectations aren't a GCSE grade. They're the small steps you're working on this term, the EHCP outcomes they lead to, and what success will look like by the next review. Say them in plain language, once, clearly.

**Show progress against it.** "She's doing well" isn't clear. "She can now count to 20 and back from 10, and she's working towards counting in twos" is. Small steps make progress visible for pupils whose progress is easily hidden by broad levels, and that visibility is what families value.

**Make sure the good news arrives too.** If most of your contact with a family is about incidents, the relationship is running on the reactive pattern the research describes. Plan regular, routine updates about learning, so that a phone call from school doesn't automatically mean bad news.

**Treat transitions as planning conversations.** Options, moving key stage and post-16 choices are where the report's largest SEN association appeared. Start those conversations early, in a format the family can take part in, and include the young person's own views.

**Don't measure engagement by volume.** A family you hear from every week may be a family under strain, not a highly engaged one. Look at whether they understand what their child is working on and how it's going, not at how many emails have gone back and forth.

## Try this tomorrow

1. **Pick three pupils with SEND** and ask yourself: could their parents say, in one sentence, what their child is working on this term and how it's going? If not, start there.
2. **Look at your last ten contacts with families** of pupils with SEND. How many were about a problem? Plan one routine, positive progress update for each.
3. **Check your next review invitations.** Do they tell families what will be discussed, and what progress has been made, before the meeting? A clear picture beforehand makes for a better conversation on the day.

## A final thought

The headline from this research isn't "contact families more". It's that families need a clear picture of what their child is working towards, and how they're getting on. For pupils whose progress comes in small steps, that picture is ours to make visible.

> "Families need a clear picture of what their child is working towards, and how they're getting on."
