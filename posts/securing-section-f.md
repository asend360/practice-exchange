## Where the gap opens up

I have worked in specialist education for many years, as a Headteacher, Executive Headteacher and Regional Executive Headteacher, leading and overseeing schools and colleges across England, Scotland and Wales. Today, as a Quality Improvement Partner, much of my work involves looking beyond policies and documentation to establish what pupils actually experience. I spend a lot of time in classrooms, talking with teachers, looking at pupils’ work and reviewing EHCPs.

My interest has always been in closing the gap between what schools intend to provide and what children actually receive. Section F is where that gap shows most clearly.

I would read detailed EHCPs setting out very specific needs and provision, then visit lessons where there was little evidence that this information had shaped the teaching. Teachers were often working incredibly hard, but they couldn’t always explain the provision specified for individual pupils or show how it was being delivered. Sometimes the SENCo knew the EHCP exceptionally well, but that knowledge hadn’t reached the classroom.

Pupils’ work over time made it especially clear. The same tasks, worksheets and recording expectations were being used for pupils with very different learning profiles. If Section F describes the educational provision a child needs, why isn’t it consistently shaping their everyday learning?

## Where Section F lives, and what gets lost

Too often, Section F lives with the SENCo. It sits in the EHCP, perhaps alongside a provision map or pupil profile, and comes up at annual reviews. But having access to an EHCP is not the same as understanding it, and understanding it is not the same as implementing it.

Teachers usually know a pupil’s diagnosis, general difficulties or behaviour, but are less confident explaining the specific provision required. Teaching assistants may know what works for a child through experience, but that knowledge isn’t always connected to the EHCP. Provision then depends on individual staff rather than being embedded in how the school teaches.

What gets lost is the detail, the specificity and the accountability. Section F may specify structured teaching approaches, visual supports, language strategies, particular interventions or specialist input. By the time it reaches the classroom, it has often become “differentiate work”, “provide adult support” or “use visual aids”. Those phrases sound right, but they don’t tell a teacher what to do.

Take a pupil whose EHCP identifies significant difficulty processing language and retaining verbal information, with provision for simplified instructions, extra processing time, visual sequencing and regular checks for understanding. In the lesson, the teacher gives a lengthy explanation to the whole class and moves quickly into the activity. When the pupil doesn’t start, a TA repeats the instructions or completes part of the task alongside them. The child has received support, but not the provision described in their EHCP. The support is reactive rather than planned.

Or a pupil whose EHCP identifies difficulties with writing, organisation or working memory, whose books show repeated expectations to copy large amounts of text or complete extended writing without scaffolding.

These are rarely examples of teachers being unwilling. More often, the school hasn’t translated the EHCP into clear enough classroom expectations. Section F provision should shape the design and delivery of learning, not just the response when learning becomes difficult.

## Why it matters

For pupils, the biggest risk is that their needs aren’t consistently met, affecting access to learning, achievement, confidence and wellbeing. We also need to recognise the link between unmet needs and behaviour: a pupil who repeatedly can’t understand instructions or access tasks may eventually disengage or become distressed.

For teachers, the risk is being expected to meet complex needs without clear guidance, resources or training. For SENCos, it creates an enormous burden, with responsibility for implementation concentrated in one person.

For leaders, there are real accountability implications. Section F specifies special educational provision, and under Section 42 of the Children and Families Act 2014, the local authority has the statutory duty to secure it. Schools have their own duties too, including those relating to SEND support, reasonable adjustments and, where applicable, placement arrangements. A school should be able to show how it contributes to delivering the specified provision, rather than relying on assurances that support is in place.

Ultimately, the greatest risk is that a child has a legally recognised entitlement that isn’t being translated into their everyday experience.

## What good looks like

When this works well, Section F is evident in planning, teaching, assessment and the classroom environment. Teachers can explain the needs of the pupils they teach and, more importantly, what they do differently as a result. You might see vocabulary pre-teaching, visual sequencing, scaffolded recording, alternative ways of showing understanding, carefully structured questioning or planned chances to revisit learning. The adaptations are purposeful rather than generic.

Good practice isn’t always visually obvious, though. Sometimes it’s the way a teacher explains a concept, checks understanding, reduces cognitive demand or allows processing time. The strongest evidence is often in what teachers know, what they do and how pupils respond.

**Turning Section F into practice.** I break each provision down into three questions:

- What exactly does the EHCP require?
- What will this look like in everyday teaching and learning?
- How will we know it is happening and making a difference?

If Section F specifies visual support to aid understanding, what does that mean for this pupil? A visual timetable, task sequencing, vocabulary prompts, worked examples or visual instructions? Who provides it, when, and how will its effectiveness be reviewed? The aim is to keep the specificity of the original provision, not replace it with vague statements.

A pupil’s needs don’t disappear when they move from English to science or a practical subject, but how provision is delivered may need to look different. That thinking led me to develop subject-specific guidance to help teachers translate Section F into practical approaches across the curriculum. The aim is not more paperwork: it’s making existing statutory provision understandable, actionable and consistent.

## Who owns it

Everyone has a role, but responsibilities must be clearly defined:

- **The SENCo** provides specialist leadership, coordinates provision, advises colleagues and supports monitoring.
- **Class teachers** are responsible for the teaching and learning of every pupil in their class, including pupils with SEND, and need to understand and implement the relevant provision.
- **Teaching assistants** support delivery, observe how pupils respond and feed back, but shouldn’t become the default owners of pupils’ learning.
- **Senior leaders** make sure staff have the time, training, resources and systems to implement provision consistently.
- **Middle and curriculum leaders** matter too: Section F can’t sit outside curriculum development and quality assurance.

If implementation depends on the SENCo checking everything, it won’t be sustainable.

## Showing it’s delivered, not just planned

I use a triangulated approach. First, look at what the EHCP specifies and how it has been translated into planning or guidance for staff. Second, observe classrooms: is the provision being delivered where and when it’s needed? Third, look at pupils’ work, assessment and progress over time. Conversations with pupils, staff and parents add another dimension.

If a pupil needs alternative recording methods, you should see them being used, not a book full of incomplete written tasks. If an intervention is specified, there should be evidence of its delivery, frequency and review. But be wary of excessive recording systems. Not every adaptation needs a written record every time. The aim is to show effective implementation, not manufacture evidence for inspection.

The common mistakes I see when schools start are: creating another document without changing practice; reducing detailed provision to phrases like “visual learner” or “needs support”; putting too much on TAs or the SENCo; focusing on whether provision is recorded rather than whether it’s effective; and assuming that because a strategy is available, it’s being used. A visual timetable on the wall isn’t evidence that a pupil is being taught to use it.

Getting this right also makes annual reviews more meaningful. Schools can explain which approaches were used, how consistently, and what difference they made. It gives parents transparency, because they shouldn’t have to keep asking whether their child’s provision is being delivered, and gives local authorities clearer evidence for discussions about provision and amendments. Crucially, it separates provision that was delivered but isn’t working from provision that wasn’t delivered as specified.

## Try this tomorrow

- **Follow one EHCP into the classroom.** Read Section F alongside the pupil’s actual experience and ask whether each provision can genuinely be seen in teaching, learning and support.
- **Translate provision into clear expectations.** Replace “differentiate appropriately” with what needs to happen, in which contexts, and how staff should adapt.
- **Talk to teachers about implementation.** Look at planning, pupils’ work and practice together, as a developmental conversation rather than a compliance check. If a teacher can’t explain how a provision is delivered, that’s an opportunity for support, not blame.

## The real measure

Having an EHCP is not the same as implementing one. Section F shouldn’t belong to the SENCo or only be revisited at annual review. It should inform everyday teaching, curriculum access, assessment and support.

Choose a pupil, read their Section F and follow it into the classroom. Can you see it? Can teachers explain it? Is the pupil benefiting? If the answers are unclear, that’s where improvement begins. Because the real measure of inclusion is not what we say we provide, but what children actually experience.
