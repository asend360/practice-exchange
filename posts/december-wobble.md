Every year, around the second week of December, someone in the staffroom says, “He’s just not himself at the moment.” They’re usually right, and it usually isn’t a mystery. Look at the week he’s had: a rehearsal in the hall instead of PE, a supply teacher on Tuesday, a Christmas jumper day nobody mentioned until the night before, and paper chains suddenly hanging over his table.

For lots of children, December is the best bit of the year. For children who rely on knowing what’s coming, it can be the hardest stretch of the autumn term. Even pupils who’ve coped well since September can start to wobble. I’ve stopped reading that as going backwards. Usually, the day has just stopped being predictable.

## Which changes cause the most wobbles

In my experience, it’s rarely one big event. It’s the stacking up. The ones I see most often:

- Christmas play rehearsals, which move lessons, take over the hall and involve lots of waiting and noise.
- Timetable swaps: PE disappears, assembly moves, a lesson happens in another room.
- Different adults: supply cover, visitors, or a familiar TA pulled into rehearsals.
- Sensory changes: decorations, music, costumes, a hall full of parents.
- Jumper days, party days and non-uniform days, which change the morning before the child has even left the house.
- Christmas lunch, with crackers, hats, a different queue and a much louder dinner hall.

Any one of these might be fine. Four in one week, with tired children and tired adults, is a different story.

## What I do in the weeks before

I start in mid-November, earlier than feels necessary. I ask the office for the December diary as soon as it exists and put every change in one place, knowing some dates will move. Then I do three things.

**I make the visual timetable tell the truth.** If it still says PE on Thursday when we’ll be rehearsing in the hall, it isn’t helping anyone. For December, I often add a simple week view, so children can see beyond today.

**I write a short social story for the biggest one or two changes.** I base mine on Carol Gray’s Social Stories™ approach, which means describing what will happen, calmly and from the child’s point of view, rather than writing a list of rules. Something like: “On Thursday, my class will go to the hall to practise the Christmas play. The hall might be noisy. Some children will sing. My teacher will be there too.” I also put in a line for when things move: “Sometimes the plan changes. If it does, an adult will tell me.”

**I walk it through.** Seeing the empty hall, trying on the costume or sitting where they’ll sit for the play answers questions a story can’t.

## What that looks like in practice

These are composites, not individual children.

A Year 2 pupil found the hall overwhelming during rehearsals. Before the first one, his TA took him into the empty hall, showed him where our class would sit and where the door was, and they read his story together. His story mentioned the loud clapping and his ear defenders, which were already part of his plan. We also gave him a job: handing out the song sheets. He still found it loud, but he knew what was coming.

A Year 5 girl was dreading Christmas lunch. It wasn’t the food, it was the crackers. Once we knew that, the fix was small. Her story said that some children pull crackers and that crackers make a bang. She chose a seat at the end of the table, and a TA pulled one with her in the classroom the day before, so the sound was no longer a surprise.

Neither of those took long. The hard part was finding out what the worry actually was.

## When the plan falls apart on the day

It will. Rehearsal moves to the morning, or the supply teacher doesn’t arrive. You can’t prepare for everything, but you can prepare children for change itself.

I teach a “change” card in November, when the stakes are low, using small swaps like a different song at carpet time. Then, when something big shifts in December, the card already means something.

In the moment, I keep words to a minimum: what’s changing, what’s staying the same, and what happens next. “Rehearsal is now. Lunch is still at twelve. Then we’re back in class.” Where I can, I lower demands and make sure the child knows where their calm space is. Afterwards, I jot down what happened for next year.

## Bringing families in

Families know their child far better than I do, so I ask before I tell: what’s December usually like at home? Then I share the dates early, not in a newsletter the night before. If I’ve written a story, I send it home, so parents and carers can read the same words in the same calm way. When home and school tell the same story, the child isn’t trying to make two versions fit together.

For jumper days and non-uniform days, a week’s notice and a clear note that uniform is always fine can save a lot of tears at the front door. If you want to use photos of the hall or staff in anything going home, check your school’s policy and your DPO first.

## If you do one thing differently this December

Put December on the timetable before December arrives. Sit down in November, list the changes for the children you’re most worried about, and prepare them for the top two. Show the change, describe it simply and let them return to it as often as they need.

I keep a list of these tricky moments across the year, and I’ve put that list into a [free Tricky Moments Calendar](https://readystories.com/free-calendar) if it’s useful. But honestly, a pencil and the school diary will get you most of the way.

> “Put December on the timetable before December arrives.”
